title: "It Doesn't Take Time. It Takes Work."
date: 2026-08-10
---

We say "it takes time" so often that we barely notice what the phrase implies.

It takes time to learn Chinese. It takes time to understand a difficult subject. It takes time to build a company. It takes time to become good at anything.

But time does not do the work.

Six months can pass without making you any better at Chinese. Someone else can get further in six weeks, because they completed more useful repetitions, tested themselves more honestly, and corrected their mistakes faster.

When we say learning Chinese takes six years, we make time sound like the active ingredient. It creates the vague idea that if you stay near the language long enough, fluency will eventually happen to you. The calendar becomes a kind of magic.

Nobody literally believes the calendar teaches Chinese. But language determines what we count. When the unit is months, we count months. When the unit is retained words, we count retained words.

That is a much better mental model.

Suppose your first meaningful milestone in Chinese is 500 common words. That is not fluency, but it is a useful foundation. For each word, you might require that you can recognize it by ear, pronounce it intelligibly, recall it when you need it, recognize its written form, and use it in a sentence. Whether you must write it by hand depends on your goal.

The objective is no longer "study Chinese for six months."

The objective is to learn and retain 500 words.

At five retained words per learning day, you are 100 productive days away. Not 100 calendar days. If you do nothing for two weeks, you are not two weeks closer. You may be further away, because you have forgotten some of what you learned.

More importantly, you can test the plan.

After ten days, you should know 50 words. Test all 50 without convenient hints. Test them again after enough delay that you are measuring memory, not temporary familiarity. If you retain only 35, your actual rate is 3.5 words per day, not five. Waiting another ten days will not fix that. You need to change the work: more review, better examples, spaced recall, real conversations.

If you retain all 50 easily, five words per day may be too conservative.

The rate can also change as you go. The first words are hard, because the sounds, tones, and writing system are unfamiliar. Later words connect to patterns you already recognize, so learning may accelerate. On the other hand, the growing burden of review may slow you down. Either way, you update the plan from evidence.

This is why even "measure the effort" is not precise enough. Effort can be wasted. Ten hours of vague studying is still not a plan. The useful unit is verified progress.

A word counts when you can use it. A software feature counts when it works. A technical risk counts when it has been tested and resolved. A sales hypothesis counts when the market has answered it.

The calendar estimate should be derived from those units:

Estimated calendar time = remaining verified work ÷ demonstrated throughput + unavoidable latency

Six months is the output of the model. It is not an input, and it is not the plan.

This applies to nearly everything.

You do not become strong because three months pass. You become strong because you complete enough progressively difficult repetitions and recover between them.

You do not improve at sales because you spend a year in sales. You improve by making calls, observing what works, reviewing failures, testing new approaches, and repeating the cycle.

You do not finish software because a team has been assigned to it for six months. You finish it by resolving uncertainties, making decisions, implementing features, testing integrations, and fixing defects.

Elapsed time counts productive and unproductive days equally. That makes it a terrible measure of progress.

It also explains three familiar project failures.

Parkinson's law says work expands to fill the time available. Give a team six months and the project mysteriously becomes a six-month project. Scope, ceremony, meetings, and polishing expand to consume the calendar.

Student syndrome is different. People delay the difficult work until the deadline becomes urgent, then try to compress everything into a final sprint.

Bikeshedding is different again. People focus on the easy, familiar, low-stakes parts of a problem, because those are the parts everyone can understand and discuss. A committee may approve a nuclear reactor in minutes, then debate the color of its bike shed for an hour. The hard thing remains untouched while everyone appears busy.

A work-based plan makes these evasions visible. "We have worked on it for three months" tells me almost nothing. Which risks have been eliminated? Which parts work? What has been tested? What remains unknown?

The nuclear reactor does not care how long the committee has been meeting.

None of this makes deadlines useless. Deadlines coordinate commitments, constrain scope, and force prioritization. They tell customers, partners, and teams when a result should be available.

But a deadline is not an execution model.

Some things also contain genuine, irreducible latency. Brains need sleep to consolidate memories. Spaced retrieval requires spacing. Bodies require recovery. Paint needs to dry. Crops need to grow. Experiments, approvals, and markets may need time to produce an answer.

That waiting belongs in the estimate. But it should not be confused with the work. Time can be a constraint. It is rarely a plan.

So when someone says a goal will take six months, ask:

What must be true at the end?  
What units of work will make it true?  
How will we verify that each unit produced real progress?  
What is our demonstrated completion rate?  
What latency genuinely cannot be compressed?

Only then should we calculate the date.

Deadlines tell us when the result is owed. The work tells us how to produce it.

Time will pass either way. Count what changes.
